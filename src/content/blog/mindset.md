Today, I got a rejection call from a recruiter.

During my career break, this was the kind of thing that would have sent me on a spiral. Today, I collected my thoughts, thanked her for her time and her feedback — any jobseeker these days knows that isn't a given — and looked forward. She was recruiting for other roles, and my career is going to persist for decades.

At this point in my journey toward finding the next right challenge, I've been in every situation under the sun. I've gotten verbals. I've gotten writtens with no start date. I've applied and found out months later that the team was already full. I even passed six rounds just to have my wifi crap out on me in the founder interview. Not exactly a fairy tale — unless we're describing the original German versions.

I'm writing this to go over some of the things that have helped me grow as someone navigating the strange tech world we live in.

Here was my usual path:

1. I didn't get the role
2. I didn't do enough
3. I'm not enough

Spending time on the other side of the table — as the staffer — changed how I read that list. Hiring managers are incredibly picky right now. If you don't meet the specific requirement, and they can't plug you in and go, the current speed of things just might not set you up to succeed. That isn't a referendum on your worth. It's a mismatch between a narrow slot and a particular week in a particular market.

None of that is an indictment on you.

So what *can* you do? That's where things shift. I don't misrepresent my background — but I can realign which skills I highlight to paint the right picture in the hirer's mind. If they have an Azure-focused data project, I can weave my years with C# and the Microsoft stack together with the problem-solving I've done in AWS. The platform is different; the muscle is the same. Sure, you can see yourself in the role — but if you can't connect those pieces for yourself, how are you going to connect them for someone else?

So my mindset is now:

1. I didn't get the role
2. What can I do differently now and going forward?
3. I'm still good — they just didn't resonate with my story

For those of you out there who resonate with this, keep workshopping your story. Who are you, why are you a fit for *this* role, and why now? A few things that have helped me answer those questions and move forward instead of spiraling:

- **Separate the outcome from the verdict.** A "no" is information about fit on a particular day, not a ruling on your worth. Write down what was actually said versus the story your brain added on top. They're rarely the same.
- **Run a quick retro, not a trial.** Ask "what would I do differently next time?" instead of "what's wrong with me?" One gives you a next move to fixate on; the other just gives you a spiral.
- **Audit your story for the role, not your résumé.** Same background, different emphasis. Figure out what this team actually needs to hear, then lead with the parts of your experience that speak to it.
- **Translate, don't inflate.** Name the underlying skill, then show it in their world — "the platform's different, the muscle's the same." You're connecting dots, not stretching the truth.
- **Convince yourself first.** If you can't honestly see yourself doing the job, no amount of polish will sell it. Build that conviction, and the pitch gets a lot easier.
- **Keep the pipeline moving.** One conversation carries far less weight when it's one of several. Momentum is its own kind of confidence.

The outcome of one call is a data point about fit, and a prompt to tell the next story a little better. The recruiter who said no today is recruiting for something else tomorrow.
